Janggi Rules at a Glance

Janggi (Korean chess) is a game for two players, Cho (楚) and Han (漢), who each try to capture the other's general. Every piece moves in its own way, and the general can never leave its palace.

1. The board and pieces

The board is made of 9 vertical and 10 horizontal lines, and the pieces sit on the points where lines cross, not inside the squares. In the middle of each side is a 3×3 area with an X drawn through it. This is the palace.

Cho and Han each have 16 pieces. Cho moves first; Han moves second and gets bonus points (komi) in return. On this site Cho is shown in green and Han in red.

2. How each piece moves

PieceMovement
GeneralMoves one point along the lines, only inside the palace. On a palace diagonal it can also move one point diagonally.
GuardMoves exactly like the general: one point along the lines inside the palace.
ChariotMoves any distance horizontally or vertically until blocked. Inside a palace it can also move along the diagonals. It is the strongest piece.
CannonMoves horizontally or vertically, but must jump over exactly one piece to do so. The piece it jumps (the screen) can be either side's, but a cannon cannot jump over another cannon, and cannons cannot capture each other. It can also jump along a palace diagonal over the piece in the center.
HorseMoves one point straight, then one point diagonally outward in the same direction.
ElephantMoves one point straight, then two points diagonally outward. It reaches farther than the horse but is blocked more easily.
SoldierMoves one point forward or sideways, never backward. Inside the enemy palace it can also move forward along the diagonals.

There is no river. Unlike Chinese chess (xiangqi), the Janggi board has no river in the middle, so every piece except the general and guards can travel anywhere on the board.

3. Blocking (myeok)

A horse or elephant cannot move if another piece stands on the path to its destination. In Korean this is called myeok, and the piece is said to be blocked.

  • Horse: if the first straight step is occupied, it cannot move in that direction.
  • Elephant: if either the straight step or the first diagonal step is occupied, it cannot move that way.

Blocking is very useful in defense. A single piece placed on the path of an enemy horse can shut it down.

4. Check and checkmate

When a piece threatens to capture the enemy general on the next move, it is check (janggun). The side in check must get out of it, and there are three ways to do so:

  1. Move the general to a safe point.
  2. Block the line between the checking piece and the general. Against a cannon, you can also remove its screen or add a second piece in between.
  3. Capture the checking piece.

If none of the three is possible, it is checkmate (oetong), and the side that is checkmated loses. On this site a red ring appears around a general in check, and only moves that escape the check are allowed.

5. Passing

In Janggi you may skip your turn when you have no good move. This is called a pass. On this site the Pass button is enabled only when the rules allow it.

6. Bikjang

When the two generals face each other on the same file with no pieces between them, it is called bikjang. How bikjang is handled differs a little between tournaments and regions. This site follows the Janggi rules of the Fairy-Stockfish engine: if bikjang is not broken, the game ends and is decided on points as shown below.

7. Points and komi

When a game has to be decided on points, as with bikjang, each side adds up the value of its remaining pieces. Players also use these values to judge the position during a game.

Han, who moves second, receives 1.5 points of komi and starts with 73.5. Because of the half point, a game decided on points can never be a draw. You can see the current material score under the evaluation bar on the game screen.
